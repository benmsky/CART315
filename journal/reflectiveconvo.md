What stuck with me most in this interview was Schön's idea that designing is a conversation with your materials. He says a designer almost never has the whole thing figured out in their head first and then just copies it out. You make a move, the material gives you something back that you didn't expect, and then you react to that. He calls this backtalk, the moment where you go "Wow, what was that?" and have to decide if the surprise is a problem or something interesting. That is pretty much how I learned Photoshop. Last semester I was trying to get some skyscrapers to resemble quartz crystals, and I had the look in my head but no idea how to get there. I spent a whole day just trying out a bunch of random tools and adjustments to see what each one would do. Then I came across something as simple as Bevel and Emboss, and it gave me exactly the look I was thinking of. I didn't get that from a tutorial. I got it because I kept making moves and paying attention to what the program gave back, and now it's a trick I can use on purpose.

Schön also talks about how you stop noticing a tool when it works well, like a pen you only think about once it runs out of ink. He says that when something fails, that is when you finally see its insides and what it is made of. For me that was my PC. It has had so many issues and so many problems, and I have pulled my own hair out trying to make it work. But I've learned a lot about how most computers work just from troubleshooting it. If it had always run fine, I probably wouldn't know half of what I know now, because I would never have had a reason to look inside.

The part about the student and his sugar bowl made me think of Destiny 2. He loved the bowl but had a hard time saying why, and the things he listed never fully explained it. I'm the same way with that game. It's hard to explain what has kept my attention for so long. I can point to things, like the skyboxes and environments, how good the guns feel to shoot, and the music. I usually end up wandering off to look around instead of doing the objective. But even when I list all of that, it doesn't really add up to the feeling of the whole thing. Schön says the overall impression of an object is separate from its parts, and that makes sense to me. As someone who wants to make environments, I think that feeling is what I'm trying to learn how to build.
