In "The Anatomy of Prototypes," Lim, Stolterman and Tenenberg argue that a prototype isn't only there to test if a design works. It's also how designers explore what a design could be. Their line that "A primary strength of a prototype is in its incompleteness" got me looking back at my own unfinished projects.

They describe two sides of a prototype. As a filter, it only shows the parts of an idea you want to look at, like appearance, data, functionality, interactivity or spatial structure. As a manifestation, it's the idea in an actual form, so you have to decide on its material, its resolution (how detailed it is) and its scope (how much of the design it covers). They also say the best prototype is the simplest one that still shows what an idea can and can't do.

My walking sim is a good example of this. It's made of basic shapes, there's one floor and you can't open the cellar, but it still shows the fog, lighting and sound.

My Pong project is similar. Pongball is a lot smaller than the moth version, but it's what let me actually finish a game.

The reading also says the dimensions are all tied together. Their example is the iPod, where the amount of music it held led to the wheel, and the wheel led to how it looks. Metal Gear Snake has a tiny version of that. Outlining the tiles sounds like an appearance change, but I'd be doing it to make turns easier to time, so it's really about interactivity.

The house study was the most interesting part for me. One group looked at a blueprint and the other walked through a 3D model of the same house. The 3D group talked about how the house felt but couldn't draw the layout afterwards, while the blueprint group drew it accurately and had ideas for remodeling it. The authors suggest that a 3D model feels more finished, while a blueprint feels open to change. That makes me wonder what people take away from my cabin. They might remember the fog and the thumping under the trapdoor without really knowing how the rooms connect, and they might not think to suggest changes because it already feels like a real place.

Next time I make a prototype, I want to decide what it's for before I build it, and what I'm leaving out on purpose.
